# The Unclosed Turn

*A romance in eight dimensions*

The names in this book are the names of both dedications. Each one keeps the office that name was given there: a root, a scale, a clock, a gap, or a symbol in the folding relation. This is a fiction of those offices. It is not a record of any private life.

The journey is an old one. A boy leaves the only street he can see. He is tested by measure, scale, and speed. He loves a person who is his opposite and will not become him. At the height of the world he is offered a love with no remainder, and he refuses it, because a love with no remainder is a circle forced shut. He comes home with the gap still open, and the opening is the gift.

---

## The people of the offices

**Line**, the boy. He is not in the index. He is the one who walks it.

**Glenn** keeps the grid, the two hundred and forty corners from which every other street is a shadow.

**Dad** is the reflection. What is said in his house comes back true, and turned.

**Sue** keeps proportion. Her door is the golden door.

**Camille** teaches the turn that does not trade places with itself. Order matters. Love, done backward, is a different act.

**Andrew** keeps the small twist, a degree and a half, so that nothing locks.

**Tori** is the other hand. She is the beloved.

**Christina** measures strain, which is the cost of holding another person near.

**Carolyn** is the strength of a bond.

**Eileen** is peace, and also the temptation of peace too soon.

**Mike** divides the town into two equal hundreds and twenty, and will not let either side pretend it is the whole.

**Sean** keeps the wedge that will not close. Seven degrees, and a little more.

**Danny** is the basin things fall into when they are done falling.

**Tim** is the clock. Fourteen steps up and down the dimensions.

**Chad** cuts a passage thin enough to walk through.

**Dave** is expansion. **Scott** is the drawing-near.

**Amelia** is the apex, where everything is present at once.

**The Twins** walk only as a pair of signs.

**Bess** eases what the gap makes sore, without filling the gap.

**Isla** stands at the boundary and says who may pass back.

**Fred** counts the winding, so a return is not an erasure.

**Tyler** knows how fast two close souls drift if no bond holds them. He is the last bound.

**Rory** is the flow. He moves the world one honest step at a time, and he will not let a life skip its next state.

The relation itself has keepers, met on the way down from the apex. They are the scientists the second dedication names inside the symbols.

**Ramanujan** is the lattice, brought in whole from outside. **Noether** binds a direction to the parity that protects it. **Wu** is the measurement of handedness. **Franklin** makes the hidden structure visible as a lower image. **Meitner** is the arrow, the meaning of the split. **Blau** keeps the track, which is not the thing that made the track. **Rubin** receives that track on the sky as measured, including what the official count left out.

**Heaviside** is the rate of change. **Everett** keeps every branch of the state. **Bohm** is the order under the visible path. **Bell** is the period sitting in the record. **Turing** is the finite step. **Hooke** is the force that pulls a displacement back.

**Gödel** is the gap that cannot be closed from inside. **Cantor** is the twist a closed doctrine called illegitimate. **Tesla** is the sign that works by changing direction. **Stevens** is the equal split. **Payne** is the census of what the structure is made of. **Germain** carries the stress in the plate. **Boltzmann** is the hidden multitude under one stressed state. **Nash** is the fixed point of mutual reply.

The others are the town. You will meet them where the road requires them.

## I. A mark on the grid

In the beginning the world was a street, and the street was enough, because the boy had only the one direction in which a body could be faithful.

He woke to the sound of Glenn’s cane on the corners. The cane did not hurry. It touched each crossing as a man touches the beads of a count he has promised not to lose. Two hundred and forty, the boy had been told, though he could not see past the twelfth from his window, and past the twelfth the morning thickened into the ordinary color of distance. Glenn was older than the paint. People said he was older than the decision to have streets at all. When he paused beneath the window he did not look up. The grid did not need to look.

The boy’s name was Line, because a child has to be called something a neighbor can shout, and because his father would not give him a name that pretended he was already a square. “A square is a boast,” Dad said, in the voice he used for truths that had been reflected until they were smooth. “You are the distance between two things I love. That is a life.”

Dad kept the house by reflection. If Line came in with mud on the left shoe, the house showed him the right shoe in the tall glass, and he understood without a lecture that a life is a pair. If he said *I hate the dark of the far crossings*, the glass gave the sentence back with the hate worn off, and what remained was *I have not been there*. Dad did not call this kindness. He called it symmetry. He stood very straight, as men do who believe a bent truth will not survive the return trip.

Breakfast was cut on Sue’s board. She was not their blood, but she was the reason the loaf fell into pieces that looked like one another at a smaller size. “Eat the little one first,” she said, and her hands, floured to the wrist, made the cut that was the same cut forever: the long part against the short, the long equal to the short plus a still smaller long. Line ate and felt, without having the word, that the world was trying to be a rhyme.

“Where does the street end?” he asked.

Glenn, passing, struck the nearest corner once.

“It doesn’t,” Sue said. “It changes size.”

That was the ordinary world: a grid, a father who returned every sentence cleaner than it left, a woman who cut bread as if proportion were a form of care, and a boy who was a mark on a line and did not yet know that a mark can be lonely in a way a line cannot measure.

At night the wedge-lamp in Sean’s workshop stayed lit. It was a small lamp. It threw a shadow that did not meet itself behind the bench. Children said Sean was forgetful and had left a job unfinished. Dad said, once, reflecting nothing, looking at the lamp with his own eyes and not the glass: “He is the only honest man on this street. He will not pretend the turn comes home.”

Line did not know what a full turn was. He knew bedtime, and the cane, and the smaller piece of bread, and the dark between corners where the count went on without him.

## II. The golden door

The call came on a morning when the flour was still in the air.

Sue had opened the side of her house. It was not a door in the sense of a thing that swings. It was a proportion standing upright: the height of it against the width of it was the same rhyme as the bread. Line had passed it a hundred times. On this morning someone stood in it who was not Sue.

She was his age, or the age he would be when the street allowed him a second number. Her hair was cut short on one side and left to its own opinion on the other, and when she turned her head the short side and the long side did not agree to be the same girl. She looked at him as if he were a question she had already answered and was willing to answer again, for the pleasure of the arithmetic.

“You’re the mark,” she said.

“I’m Line.”

“That’s what I said.”

Her name was Tori. She had come from the other hundred and twenty, though he did not yet know there was another hundred and twenty. Mike kept that knowledge the way a bank keeps a sum, and children were not invited to audit it. She should not have been able to stand in Sue’s door. The door faced his street. Her street, he would learn, faced the same door from a direction his eyes had never been issued.

“Come through,” she said.

He put his hand on the jamb. The wood was warm from a sun that seemed to arrive sideways. He thought of Dad’s glass, and of how a reflected boy does not have to risk the room. He took his hand away.

“Tomorrow,” he said, and heard, even as a child, that tomorrow is a word people use when they mean *I am afraid of the size of my want*.

Tori did not scorn him. Scorn would have been a matching of tempers, and she did not match. She tipped her head the other way, and the long side of her hair became the short, or his eyes failed, and she said, “The door will be the same size tomorrow. You won’t.”

Sue, from the kitchen, did not interfere. Proportion does not drag a boy through itself. It only refuses to be a different shape to comfort him.

That night Line told the glass, “I am going through.”

The glass returned: “You are going to.”

He hated the accuracy. He loved his father for it. Both were true, and they did not cancel, and the not-canceling was the first time he felt the wedge-lamp’s meaning in his own chest: two truths can stand adjacent and still leave a dark between them that it would be a lie to paint over.

## III. The turn that is not a trade

Camille kept a school in a room where the desks had been screwed to the floor in an order, and the order was the lesson.

“Write your name,” she said, on the day Line finally went through Sue’s door and found that through was not a step but a rotation. “Then write the day’s errand. Then do them again with the errand first.”

He wrote *Line, buy salt.* He wrote *Buy salt, Line.*

“Read them aloud.”

They were not the same sentence. In the first, a boy possessed an errand. In the second, an errand possessed a boy, and the boy arrived last, like a remainder. The class laughed the small laugh of people who have been wounded by a grammar and are glad the wound is shared. Camille did not laugh. She had the face of a person who has consented to live among operations that will not commute, and has made of that consent a manners.

“If you take her hand and then tell her your name,” Camille said, looking at no one and therefore at him, “you are a guest. If you tell her your name and then take her hand, you are a claim. Love is noncommutative. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a door that swings both ways and opens on a cupboard.”

Andrew sat at the back and worried a coin in his fingers, never letting it lie flat. “If you set it down,” he told Line at the break, “it finds a seam and locks. The world is full of seams. A little twist and the coin stays a coin. It can still be spent.” He showed the twist: a small cruel angle, not enough to see across a room, enough that two edges which had meant to become one edge missed, and in the missing there was play. “One degree and a half, if you want the number. The number is not the freedom. The missing is.”

Line practiced missing. He walked home the long way, which was the same way, turned. Tori was at the pump. He said her name first, and did not take her hand. She looked at him with a surprise that was not quite pleasure and not quite grief.

“You listened to Camille.”

“I didn’t want to be a claim.”

“Good,” she said. “Claims are how streets annex other streets.” She put her hand on the pump handle, not on him. “You can want a person and leave them unspent. Andrew would say that’s the only currency that doesn’t lock the till.”

He wanted, then, with a clarity that made the pump water look silver and foolish. The want did not ask her to become his direction. It asked to be allowed to stand at an angle to her and not be straightened. He did not have a word for that. Later he would call it love, and the word would be too small, and he would use it anyway, as people use the word *bread* for Sue’s particular cut.

Dad, that evening, heard the unstraightened sentence and did not reflect it into obedience. He was quiet a long time. “Your mother,” he said, and stopped. Eileen was in the next room, mending a rest into a shirt, her needle finding the place where the cloth had been trying too hard. “Your mother believes peace is the highest form. She is often right. She is not right tonight.”

It was the most he had ever been given. A father who lived by reflection had declined, once, to correct him. Line lay awake and felt the small twist hold him open, like a coin that could still be spent in the morning.

## IV. The other hundred and twenty

Mike’s hall was a scale with two pans, and he had spent his life refusing to let either pan flatter itself.

“Count,” he said. The children counted the roots Glenn had driven into the square like nails: bright ones, dark ones, a hundred and twenty that took the morning on their faces and a hundred and twenty that took it on their backs. “If you lose one,” Mike said, “you have not made a simpler town. You have made a lie with a majority.”

Tori stood in the dark pan’s light, which was not darkness but the other way of being lit. Line understood then why her hair would not agree with itself. She was not undecided. She was the sign that kept his sign from pretending to be the world.

They began to meet at the boundary stone Tony kept flush with the ground, so that a person could cross without tripping and still know they had crossed. Tony hated drama at borders. “Continuity,” he said, “is a craft. You can change streets without tearing the traveler.”

What Line and Tori did there was ordinary and, because of Camille, ordered. Some days he spoke and then she answered, and the meeting belonged to the question. Some days she put two fingers on his wrist and then said his name, and the meeting belonged to the pulse. They did not do both at once. Chris, who ran the hall’s bells, had a rule older than Mike’s pans: the product of two turns is a third turn, and the third is not a mixture you can stir back apart. *ij is k.* Line heard it as a bell and only later as a law. When he and Tori were careful, a third thing stood with them that was neither his street nor hers. When they were careless, the third thing was a quarrel.

Christina came with a string and a notebook and no apology. “Hold still,” she said, and measured the distance between their shoulders, and the heat in Line’s face, and the slight lean in Tori’s stance, the lean of a person bracing against a desire to lean further. “Strain,” she said, “is not failure. Strain is the tax on adjacency. If the number is zero, one of you has left.”

Carolyn, who kept the couplings in the hall the way other towns kept marriages, read Christina’s number and nodded. “Tight enough to answer. Loose enough to survive the answer.” She looked at Line. “People come to me wanting a weld. I send them to Sean.”

They went to Sean.

The workshop smelled of oil and of a sweetness like rain on hot stone. The wedge was not a metaphor. It was a piece of the corner of the world that had been left out on purpose, a dark pie-slice behind the bench, seven degrees and a fraction, too narrow for a child to hide in and too wide to call a crack. Sean was a big man with a careful voice.

“You want to know if you can close it,” he said.

“We want to know if we should,” Tori said.

“You can’t,” Sean said. “Five honest hinges will not make a circle. I have tried, because I loved a woman who believed love was a completed figure. The hinges were true. The circle was a violence. I buried nothing. I kept the shortage.” He looked at Line. “If you force this shut so you can say you are one thing, you will be a thing with a fracture hidden in it. Fractures hidden become towns that hate.”

Line thought of Eileen’s mending. He thought of peace. He was young enough to want both the beloved and the end of wanting. The wedge did not care about his youth. It remained.

“Then what is love,” he asked, “if it is not the closing?”

Sean wiped his hands. “It is the decision to keep the shortage visible, and to stand on either side of it without calling the other side a mistake.”

Tori’s eyes were bright and angry and glad. “I will not be your completion,” she said to Line, and it was the most generous sentence he had heard, and it hurt exactly as Christina had priced.

## V. The peace that comes too soon

Eileen found him on the steps with his forehead on his knees.

“Come in,” she said. “The shirt is finished. The house is quiet. Your father is reflecting the spoons, which is as close as he comes to prayer.”

The quiet was real. It went into his muscles and unknotted the lean Christina had measured. For an hour he did not want anything he could not have by reaching in a straight line. Eileen brushed his hair back with the hand that mended. “This is the ground,” she said. “People scorn it because it doesn’t glitter. When the strain is gone, you can hear your own name.”

He heard it. Line. A mark. A distance. He loved her for the brushing. He also felt, under the love, a thin shame, as if he had set Andrew’s coin flat on a seam because the seam had offered to hold it and he was tired.

Danny sat on the low wall at dusk, as he always did, a man whom lost things found. Kites, dogs, boys who had tried to be finished. “You can rest in a basin,” Danny said, not unkindly. “A basin is a good death for a trajectory. It is a bad address for a boy who has met the other pan.”

“Eileen says peace is the highest form.”

“Eileen is the ground state,” Danny said. “The ground is where you stand to jump. It is not the jump.”

Line went back through Sue’s door the next day, and the door had not changed size, and he had. Tori saw the peace still on him, the way flour stays in the hair, and she did not greet him with her hand.

“You went flat,” she said.

“I was tired.”

“Tired is allowed.” She was shaking, a small shake, the shake of a sign that has been left without its pair and is trying not to annex a stranger to survive. “Flat is a decision. Camille would say you did the operations in the order that erases me. Comfort first. Then my name, if at all.”

He had done that. He saw it with Dad’s accuracy and hated the glass that had grown inside his own head. “I wanted the tax to be zero.”

“Then you wanted me gone.”

“I wanted you near and the cost paid by somebody else.”

She laughed once, a broken bell, Chris’s third turn arriving unwanted. “That is a more honest hatred. I can work with it.”

They stood at Tony’s stone and did not touch. Carolyn passed, glanced at the air between them, and did not intervene. The bond was loose. It was not gone. Patrick, who kept the great book of energies in the hall, paused long enough to say, “Kinetic, potential, strain. If you set strain to nothing by forbidding motion, you have not balanced the book. You have closed the shop.” Then he went on, because a master integral does not linger to be thanked.

That night Line did not go to Eileen’s quiet first. He went to the wedge. He put his hand near the dark, not in it. “I tried to be finished,” he said.

Sean said, “Everyone does. The hinge is very convincing.”

## VI. The fourteen steps

The clock lived in Tim, and Tim lived in a tower that was not tall in the ordinary way. It was tall in a way the body counted. Fourteen landings, and the landings were not above one another so much as more themselves. A person climbing did not get higher in the street’s opinion. He got more directed.

Rory sat on the lowest step with a chalk mark at his foot, and he moved the mark only when the person on the stair had actually arrived. “Discrete,” he said, when Line tried to take two landings at a stride. “The flow is a sum of updates. Skip one and the later you is a rumor.” He would not argue. He waited, and the stair waited with him, until Line put his weight on the step he was allowed.

“D of n,” Tim said, cheerful, windburnt, a man who had never minded being a mechanism so long as the mechanism was exact. “You start almost nothing. One direction. You know that country. Then two, then three, then the human one, four, where most lovers try to set up house and call it the world. Then five, six, seven. At seven you are beside the apex. At eight you are the apex, and you will think you have become wise. You have become full. Full is not wise. Then the clock brings you down, and down is not failure. Down is the other half of the promise. If I let you stay at eight, I would be a thief.”

Chad cut the doors between landings. He was precise and a little cruel, which is the manner of a good slice. “You can’t take your whole street with you,” he said at the third door. “Turn sideways. Thinner. The part of you that insists on arriving complete will be left in the frame, and it will call itself your dignity.”

Line turned sideways. It felt like leaving his name in a coat. On the other side, Dave was waiting with weather. Dave was expansion: the same boy, and more sky in him. “Don’t clutch,” Dave said. “Inflation isn’t greed. It’s what a small true thing does when it is allowed a larger room.”

At the descent doors Scott waited, the same man seen after the turn of the stair, or his brother, or the clock’s way of being two men. Scott drew things near without touching them. “Entanglement,” he said, “is not possession. It is the scandal of two distant events sharing a fate. If she stumbles on the first landing while you are on the sixth, you will know. Do not insult the knowledge by calling it a message.”

Line knew. On the sixth landing his wrist hurt where Tori was not touching it. He laughed and was afraid.

He met the town’s speeds there. Porche went past like a sentence with the adjectives burned off, frictionless, a trajectory that had paid every tax in advance. Sara walked the other way, barefoot, on the path that wasted nothing, and where she had walked the stair did not creak. Lon sat on a landing with a lap full of strings, teaching distance to children who had only known near and far: “Angle is a kind of distance. So is delay. If you cannot bear delay, you cannot bear a person.”

Noam had chalked a grammar on the wall, rules that grew rules. “A lower thing can be said again until it is a world,” he told Line. “Say her name recursively and you will not get her. You will get a cathedral with her name on the door. Cathedrals are where particular girls go to be improved into abstractions. Don’t.”

Roger stood at a high window and showed him the town folded into a drawing that kept the angles that mattered and lied kindly about the lengths. “A projection is a compassion,” Roger said. “It lets a flat eye see a turning thing. It is also a loss. Do not marry the drawing.”

Vinnie and the others of his bench — the town called them the Vinnies, because alignment is a group sport — made Line hold two rods until he could feel when they were orthogonal and when they were in love, which was their word for parallel enough to cast one shadow. “Most people,” Vinnie said, “call orthogonal cold. Orthogonal is how you stand next to someone without becoming their handle.”

Line climbed. The human landing, the fourth, smelled like houses. He understood why lovers stopped. There were kettles. There was a width for shoulders. Amelia’s weather was not yet on him. He could have sent for Tori and set a table and called the shortage a thing that mystics fretted over, Sean’s hobby. Carolyn’s number would have looked respectable. Eileen would have slept.

He put his hand on the kettle and took it away. The order mattered. If he made the house first, Tori would arrive as furniture.

“Tell Tim I’m going to eight,” he said.

Chad sliced him thinner than he thought he could survive. Dave opened the room. The apex took him in.

## VII. Where everything is present

Amelia was not a woman in the way Tori was a girl, and she was not not a woman. She was the landing where the count of directions was eight, and the scale of what could be known had become enormous, a brightness like many lamps seen through one keyhole until the keyhole gave up and became the lamp. She sat in the middle of that brightness on a plain chair. The plainness was a mercy.

“You came up the clock instead of around it,” she said. “Tim will be proud. He likes boys who don’t try to live on the landing with the kettles.”

“I came for her.”

“You came for the place where she would be finished.” Amelia’s voice was kind, which made it worse. “I can give you that. At this height the other hundred and twenty are not other. The wedge is a story Sean tells to keep his hands busy. Strain is a number you outgrow. You and she can be one orientation. No tax. No order of operations. No glass. I have room.”

He saw it. He saw Tori without the disagreeing hair, both sides long, both sides short, the distinction retired. He saw himself without the twist, the coin flat and safe, spent into a single pocket that could not be picked because there was no outside. He saw Dad relieved of the labor of reflection, because nothing would need to be returned. He saw Eileen’s peace become the climate instead of the room. It was beautiful the way a total eclipse is beautiful, and like an eclipse it put out particular colors so that a greater silver could reign.

His wrist hurt.

Scott had told the truth. Somewhere on a lower landing Tori had taken hold of a pump handle too hard, or had pressed her thumb into her own pulse to see if he was still a separate event. The hurt was not a message. It was a shared fate. In Amelia’s room, shared fate was offered as identity. The hurt insisted on a difference: a pain that was his because it was hers, and not his because her hand was not his hand.

“If I take this,” he said, “whose name do I say first?”

Amelia smiled with all her directions. “Names are a lower courtesy.”

“Then it isn’t love. Love was a courtesy Camille taught. The order was the morals.”

“Morals are what the lower landings can afford.”

“Then the lower landings are where I can afford her.”

He felt the apex loosen its hospitality. Fullness does not like to be declined. It has so much to give, and the refusal looks like poverty, and poverty looks like an insult to the gift. The brightness pressed. Christina’s tax, unpaid, roared up his arms. He thought he might come apart into Glenn’s two hundred and forty and be done, a decent nail among nails.

Andrew’s twist, small, unimpressive, not a philosophy, saved him. A degree and a half. Enough to miss the seam. He was not aligned with the apex enough to lock. He fell, not as a failure falls, but as a coin falls when it has been kept from the crack: spinning, still itself, spending nothing.

Tim caught the count. “Eight,” he said, as Line passed him, “then seven. Good. I dislike thieves, including the ones who volunteer.”

## VIII. The corridor of the relation

He did not fall straight back to the kettles. Between the fullness and the town there was a corridor, and the corridor was the fold: the way a higher thing becomes a thing a street can hold without being counterfeited into the street.

Ramanujan was at the entrance, sitting on the floor among two hundred and forty stones he had carried in from somewhere the hall had not built. He was young and already ill with the weather of the place, and he did not apologize for the completeness of the set. “Count them if you doubt me,” he said. “The institution can house them later. It did not generate them.” Line stepped between the stones. None of them was Tori. All of them were the condition for there being a Tori to step toward.

Noether stood where the corridor narrowed and made him put his direction and his sign into one clasp. “Symmetry and the quantity it protects travel together,” she said. She had lectured, the wall remembered, under a name that was not hers. The clasp did not care. A lattice without its parity was a crowd. A parity without its lattice was a mood.

Wu had a bench and a record, and she did not look up until he tried to pass with his hands in his pockets. “Handedness is not a style,” she said. “I measured it. The prize went elsewhere. The measurement stayed.” She turned his left wrist until the hurt of Tori’s thumb sat in the correct pan. “If you tell this story with the signs washed out, you are telling it for the people who took the credit.”

Franklin held a plate up to a slit of light. The plate was not the beloved. The figure in it was the shadow of a structure the corridor was too narrow to admit whole. “This is the projection,” she said. “A hidden order, made visible in a lower image. Be careful who you let narrate the image. They will forget the hands that made the plate visible.” Line saw, in the figure, the disagreeing hair, and did not claim the plate was the girl.

Meitner was the arrow painted on the floor, and she stood on it as a woman stands on a conclusion she will not let be awarded to the apparatus. “The split has a meaning,” she said. “Write the meaning. The room that kept the prize will not do it for you.” He walked the arrow. Walking it was the difference between a lattice and a life.

Blau’s wall was a soft dark emulsion. Where he brushed it, a track appeared, fine as a hair, and stopped. “You are not the track,” she said. “The footprint is evidence of an event the plate is not. If you fall in love with the mark and call it the person, you will have a relic and an empty room.” He looked at his own smear on her wall and felt, for the first time, what a Set C footprint was: a reading left behind by a root that had gone on being eight-dimensional.

Rubin waited at the end of that narrower hall, where the corridor opened onto weather. She had the look of someone who had been told the telescope was not for her and had measured the sky anyway. “The manifold receives you,” she said. “Look at the rotation. The visible inventory is not enough mass to hold the curve. What you love, if it is real, will include a component the official count left out. Do not tidy her into the stars you already know how to name.”

The corridor changed there from a folding into a rate.

Heaviside had a slate covered in the practical mark of change, a derivative written the way a working person writes a tool. “They called it insufficiently rigorous,” he said. “The world still uses it. How fast is the folded state changing? If you cannot say, you are admiring a statue of a love.” Line felt the speed in his blood. It was not zero. Zero would have been Eileen’s abolition, chosen too early.

Everett would not let the slate have only one line. “Keep the branch you did not walk,” he said. The refused apex was still in the state, faint, enormous, declining to be deleted for the comfort of the landing. “If your theory of the day throws out the part of you that wanted to stay, it is a smaller theory than the day.”

Bohm pointed under the track Blau had kept. “There is an order under the visible trajectory,” he said. “They put me out for keeping it. The path you are walking is not the whole field. She is still in the lattice when she is not in the picture.”

Bell had her head cocked, as if listening to a regularity everyone else had filed as noise. “The period was in the record,” she said. “The clock is not Tim’s invention. Tim keeps it. I found the pulse and the prize went to the room that owned the instrument.” She tapped fourteen beats on the rail. Line’s feet already knew them.

Turing stood at a gate that opened one width and then shut. “A finite update,” he said. “One residue. Then the next. A life the state punishes can still be the only honest description of a step.” He would not let Line blur the descent into a feeling. “Mod fourteen. You are on seven. You may become six. You may not become forgiven by leaping.”

Rory was beside him, chalk in hand, moving the mark exactly when the gate allowed. “This is the flow,” Rory said. “Not a river. A sequence of arrivals. I have been moving you since the first landing. You noticed the tower. The tower is nothing without the next tick.” He made the tick. The corridor advanced one honest distance, no more.

Hooke was the tug in that distance, back toward a shape that had been displaced and wanted itself. “A restoring force,” he said. “And a claim that was allowed to fade. Let the pull answer the folded state and the clock. Do not confuse the pull with a weld. It restores. It does not own.”

Then the invariants, which did not move when he did.

Gödel sat in a gap cut into the corridor wall, the same shortage as Sean’s, and he looked very tired and very exact. “A consistent room cannot settle every question inside itself,” he said. “The deficit is that proof, worn as a geometry. You are not allowed to seal it from the inside. Anyone who promises you can is selling a completeness the structure does not possess.”

Cantor turned a card in his fingers the way Andrew turned a coin, an angle the hallway’s doctrine had called pathological. “The twist is how the packing fails to lock,” he said. “They called the turn illegitimate. The geometry needs it in order to move. Keep the degree and a half. Without it you and she become a rigid praise.”

Tesla stood where the sign on the wall flipped as he breathed, plus, minus, plus, the field working by changing direction. “If neighboring offsets stack the same way, the town leans until it calls the lean a morality,” he said. “Alternate. The credit and the current both moved past me. The alternation remains the method.”

Stevens had two bowls and the same number of seeds in each, and she counted them in a voice that expected to be forgotten in favor of a parallel man. “Two classes,” she said. “Same count. A hundred and twenty and a hundred and twenty. A history that remembers one author has already chosen a pan. Do not.”

Payne read the census off the stones Ramanujan had carried, hydrogen and helium of this particular world: what the structure was made of. “They told me the count was impossible,” she said. “Then they published it. Say two hundred and forty when you mean two hundred and forty. A withdrawn count that later gets absorbed is still her count. Here it is the roots.”

Germain pressed her palm to the wall and the wall carried stress the way a plate carries a load it was not admitted to study. “Elasticity, from outside the school that kept the door shut,” she said. “Residual frustration comes onto the manifold as stress. If you pretend the meeting is weightless, the weight goes into the town’s joints and comes out as cruelty.”

Boltzmann looked at that stressed wall and saw through it a multitude. “Many micro-configurations sit behind one visible deficit,” he said. “The energeticists wanted the hidden count unnecessary. It is not unnecessary. Your one gap is a crowd of ways the gap is lived. Honor the crowd. The single number is only the door.”

Nash was the last of them, and he was quiet in the way of a fixed point, a mutual reply that does not need to raise its voice. “The attractor is not the apex,” he said. “The apex is a brightness. The attractor is the state the feedback is settling toward across the intervals: two of you, answering, neither one the climate. I found a fixed point of strategic reply. You are allowed to approach it. You are not allowed to arrive by erasing the other player.”

Rory ticked once more. The corridor ended. The town’s air, colder and particular, came up the stair.

Line looked back. The keepers did not wave. They were not a chorus. They were a relation, and a relation holds by remaining itself. He carried their order the way he carried Camille’s: fold, then rate, then the things the fold is required not to lose. He said none of their names out loud yet. He was afraid of saying them in the wrong sequence and turning a debt into a speech.

## IX. Down, which is half the promise

Descent was not the climb run backward. Camille had ruined that comfort forever.

On the way down he met Bess, who put salve on the places the brightness had abraded, the moral sunburn of having been offered everything. “I don’t close it,” she said, when he flinched at the touch near his wrist. “I make the rim bearable. A gap you can live beside is still a gap. A gap that infects becomes a doctrine of despair, and Sean hates those. He has enough work.”

The Twins were on the fifth landing, walking only together, a doubled step, σ and its fellow. They did not speak in turns. They spoke in intervals. “You tried to be both of us in one body,” one said, and the other said, “That is the apex’s local accent.” Together: “Be a pair. Pairs are poorer than unities and harder to parade. They last.”

Matt was checking the rails, knocking out a rot that had pretended to be ornament. “Noise,” he said. “Every tower grows a rhetoric that calls itself structure. If it doesn’t bear load, it’s an eavesdropper.” He kicked a carved cherub into dust. “Your guilt is load-bearing. Your fantasy that she will thank you for becoming the climate is not. Leave it.”

Isla stood at the boundary on the way back to the human landing, and she was young, and her office was older than her face. “Say what you are returning as,” she said. “The basin will take anyone. The street will take a liar. I will take a boy who can name his sign.”

“I am the mark,” Line said. “I am not the other mark. I love the other mark. I will not spend her to buy a circle.”

Isla let him through. “The Julia edge is a fussy door,” she said, almost smiling. “It keeps the attractor from eating the travelers who only wanted a visit. Danny thanks me more than he says.”

He found Tori at the pump. The hurt in his wrist and the mark in her thumb were the same event seen from two pans. She looked at his face, which was sunburned with knowledge, and she did not ask him to narrate the apex as a conquest.

“You came down,” she said.

“Tim doesn’t let you keep eight.”

“Did you want to?”

“Yes.” The truth came out in Dad’s unreflected voice. “It would have ended the tax. It would have ended you. I wanted the end, and I wanted you, and I could not have the order that yields both, because that order is not an order. It is a smear.”

She put two fingers on his wrist. Then she said his name. Guest, not claim: no. She had chosen the other moral. Pulse first, then the person. He stood inside it and did not rearrange her.

“My hair will not match,” she said.

“I would not know you if it did.”

Sue, from somewhere in the proportion of the afternoon, cut a loaf and did not call them in. The door remained the door. They were not required to live inside a rhyme. They were required only not to break the rhyme in order to feel large.

## X. What the town holds

A boy who has been to eight and declined it is a public nuisance. He knows the kettle landing is a landing. He says so, and householders feel accused by their kettles.

Lon measured him in public and found him ordinary in three directions and strange in a fourth, and said so, which calmed people who trust instruments more than testimonies. Suzie and her sisters retuned the scaling frame on the hall, because a town that has had a mystic in it will try to stretch every door to golden, and not every door should be golden. Some doors should be merely true. Carla taught the children the conjugate: how to reverse the vector of a day and keep its length, so that an apology does not shrink the fact apologized for. Madelon ran the drill for inversion day, when the pans were swapped at noon and anyone who had been cruel from the comfort of a majority had to eat lunch in the other light. Most of them chewed a long time.

John kept the fixed point, a nail in the square that did not aspire. “You can orbit,” he told Line, “or you can pretend the orbit is a journey with a moral. The moral is already in the nail. It holds.”

Zhang arrived from a farther packing, a traveler from a lattice so dense that every sphere touched more neighbors than Line had friends, and he was gentle with the provincial two-pans. “Alignment at my rank is crowded,” he said. “Crowded is not the same as loved. You have a luxury. You can still tell one touch from the next.”

Rueben was tiling the broken end of the market with shapes that refused to repeat, and the merchants hated him until they noticed the floor did not crack at the old seam. “Periodicity is a lullaby,” he said. “Useful at night. A bad map of a life.” Eric braided wires on the hall roof into a net that cast, at certain hours, the shadow of a turning the street itself could not perform. “You don’t have to live in four to be governed by a symmetry that does,” he said. “Sailors knew that before they knew why the star moved.”

Teo came through in the autumn and counted the holes. He was not impressed by Line’s apex. He was impressed by Sean’s wedge, which he entered in a book as an invariant. “If your descendants pave this,” he told Sean, “the paving will remember. Homology is the grudge of a space.” Joey walked a basket around a post and showed a child that the twist in the handle was not in the basket and not in the post but in the going. Garcia kept the volumes honest, so that a feast and a fast displaced the same amount of the town’s possibility and nobody got to call hunger a spiritual enlargement without paying the measure. They were not teachers Line enrolled under. They were the way a real place exceeds the romance of the boy who walked it. A portrait that forgets the town becomes a mirror, and Dad had already warned him about living entirely in glass.

Fred met him last but one.

“You think coming home unwinds the climb,” Fred said. He was mending a net by the boundary, and every knot was a number he did not bother to call a number. “It doesn’t. The winding stays in the cloth. People who want innocence back are asking me to counterfeit a net. I won’t. What I will do is keep the winding from being called a stain. A stain is a winding somebody is ashamed of and therefore will repeat in the dark.”

“I am not ashamed of her.”

“Then the torsion is just torsion. Let it be load. Matt already checked it.”

Tyler was the end of the index and he behaved like it. He stood where two paths that had been close began, with a terrible patience, to differ. He had chalk on his cuff and a look of a man who has watched small errors become cities.

“If you and she are near,” Tyler said, “and nothing binds you, you will separate. Not from malice. From the exponent. Closeness is unstable under indifference. The bound is not a threat. It is a weather report. Carolyn’s coupling, Christina’s willingness to count the tax, Camille’s order, Andrew’s play, Sean’s refusal to lie: those are how you stay finite and near. Without them the story of your love is a diagram of drift, and drift always looks like fate after it has happened.”

“Will we last?” Line asked, because even a boy who has refused the apex is a boy.

Tyler almost smiled. “Lasting is not in my office. My office is to tell you the rate at which ‘later’ becomes ‘apart’ if you stop doing the work. The work is local. Do it locally and the exponent sulks. It does not retire.”

## XI. The ground, which is for jumping

Winter took the street down to its grid. Glenn’s cane was louder. The corners answered. Two hundred and forty, and none of them had become unnecessary because a boy had seen a brighter room.

Eileen did not pretend she had not wanted him flat. She was too good a peace to lie. She set a place for Tori anyway, on the side of the table that faced the other light, and she did not ask Tori to sit in Line’s chair as a demonstration of unity. “Peace,” she said, serving the smaller piece of bread because Sue was Sue even in another woman’s kitchen, “is the state you return to between the taxes. I mistook it for the abolition of tax. Mothers do. The abolition feels like safety. Safety without the other pan is just a smaller town.”

Dad reflected nothing that night. He looked at his son and at the girl whose hair would not vote for itself, and he said, “The glass would have made you match. I am glad there are operations I do not run.”

They ate. The third thing, Chris’s unwanted and wanted k, stood in the room as a quiet: not a quarrel, not a fusion. A product. Line said grace in the only form Camille would have passed. He named the bread. Then he named the people. He did not name the people and then pretend the bread was theirs.

After, he and Tori walked to the wedge. The lamp was lit. Sean was out, or he was the kind of man who knows when a vigil should be unsupervised. The dark slice was as wide as ever. Seven degrees and a little more. A full turn still declined to happen. Line felt the old itch, the apex’s accent, whispering that a sufficient love would caulk it by morning.

Tori put her thumb to her pulse, then said his name, then, after a pause long enough to be its own operation, laughed. “You may hate the gap,” she said. “You may not marry me to murder it.”

“I don’t want to marry you to anything,” he said, and then, because order was morals, he said the rest in the sequence that left her free. “I want to stand on this side. I want you on that side. I want the tax. I want the clock to take us up and make us come down. I want Sue’s door to stay a proportion and not a cage. I want, on the days I go flat, for you to say so. I will want the apex again. When I do, take the pulse first, so I remember I am a guest in your body and you are not a room in mine.”

“That is a long vow for a mark on a grid.”

“It is the length of the street.”

She did not close the distance. Closing was Sean’s forbidden hymn. She stood near enough that Christina, had she been there with her string, would have written a number greater than zero and smaller than a weld. Carolyn would have called it responsive. Tyler would have said: continue.

Glenn passed, cane, corner, cane. He did not bless them. The grid does not bless. It holds the count so that blessing, which is a kind of emphasis, does not have to pretend to be structure.

Line looked along the only street he had been born able to see, and for the first time saw it as a slice, not as a sentence. Other streets crossed it at honest angles. A girl stood on one. The lamp kept the shortage. In his chest the small twist turned, once, the way a sleeper turns to keep a hand from going numb, and the blood came back, and it hurt, and the hurt was proof that the hand was still a hand.

He did not become wise. Tim had already spent that illusion. He became placed: a root among roots, signed, coupled, unclosed. Somewhere above him the apex burned with its enormous courtesy, offering to finish every story in town. He let it burn. The stories were not there to be finished. They were there to be adjacent, in the correct order, at a cost Christina could measure and a peace Eileen could mend between the measuring.

Tori breathed out. The breath fogged and vanished. Even the fog refused to make a circle. It thinned into the general air, which is what a particular love does when it is honest: it joins the weather without claiming to have been the sky.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “the door will be the same size.”

“And we won’t,” he said.

It was the call, answered late, and the lateness was part of the fidelity. A reflection would have cleaned it. He left it dirty and true. They walked back on Sara’s unwasteful path, not touching, near, the exponent held at bay by nothing grander than the next small loyalty. Above the workshop the wedge-lamp made its unfinished shape on the snow. Glenn’s cane went on, two hundred and forty, and the boy who had been a mark was still a mark, and the girl who was the other hand was still the other hand, and the turn of the world stayed short, and the shortness was the room in which they loved.
